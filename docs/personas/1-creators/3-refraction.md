# Refraction

You wrote it once. It doesn't sit there. It goes to work.

This is where you get to do your magic for the AI era.

## Realizing what you always knew

You always knew a first-time visitor and a returning customer shouldn't see the same thing.

You knew that the static phase of design in a digital world was just a phase — a compromise, not a principle. Digital was never bound the way print was. There was no print run forcing every version into one batch, no per-copy cost making "one version for everyone" the only economical choice. That constraint was real for print. It was never real for a screen. Design just kept behaving as if it still was.

Your instinct was never wrong. It just never had a way to actually run — not at the speed real work happens, not without a team hand-building every version by hand, one persona at a time, until "three personas" quietly became "the one persona we had time to design for."

You worked in an environment where you'd lost your voice to engineering, number crunchers, and project managers. You were pushing the print paradigm through the process because that's how everyone else understood design.

## Every persona gets its variant

You know your design can be moulded to every persona, one at a time. This is well within your design's conception itself. Each variant is solving a different problem, not wearing a different skin. You were never allowed to think that far because of the *overhead* it would cause — an argument that did not come from amongst designers.

> AI makes every variant possible and achievable. Suddenly, you and your design are the constraint. It is a strange liberating feeling.

## You already think in components. Now let that thinking go further.

You already design this way — components, not one-off screens. Most tools you use are built around it. A button, a card, a nav bar: built once, reused everywhere it fits.

What changes here is what "variant" is allowed to mean. Not just a button in a different colour for a different persona — a genuinely different component, because a different persona sometimes needs a different thing entirely, not a reskinned version of the same one. The layout it sits inside can change too. Nothing about the component system you already know is being replaced. It's being allowed to go as far as you always could have taken it.

Let your imagination go where you were told it was theoretical. A version of this screen that doesn't just look different but works differently, structurally, for a persona who needs it to. That was always a legitimate design idea. It just never made it past the room where someone had to say yes.

Why does it make it past that room now? Because downstream, there's no longer a human whose job is to say no. 

An engineer weighing the build cost of a fourth variant against a sprint deadline isn't the one building it anymore. The constraint that killed the idea in the room was never a design constraint. It was a resourcing one, wearing a design conversation's clothes.

## It gets shaped, per persona

Every persona you named gets its own version — not because someone copies your document and edits it by hand, but because the document is precise enough to be reshaped automatically for whoever it's for.

This is called **refraction** — the same document, separated into what's actually relevant for each persona, the way a prism separates light into what was already there. Nothing gets added that you didn't already put in. It's your intent, sorted by who's receiving it.

## What actually changes, per persona

Five dimensions of experience get shaped differently for each persona you named:

| Dimension | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| **Surface** | What they see, and how it's arranged. Not just the screen — the order things appear in, what's most prominent, what they see first versus what's secondary. |
| **Context** | What they already know, coming in. A returning visitor and a first-time visitor aren't shown the same starting point, because they're not arriving at the same one. |
| **Content** | What's actually shown to them. The same underlying material can lead with a different piece of it, depending on who's looking. |
| **Time** | What's remembered from last time. Some personas need continuity — where they left off, what they've already seen. Some don't. |
| **Trust** | What they need to trust before they'll act. A first-time visitor and a loyal customer don't need to be convinced of the same things, in the same order. |

You already answered most of this when you said what matters for each persona. Refraction is just the name for what happens when those answers are actually put to use.

## Refining is normal — expected, even

You're not locked into what you wrote the first time. If a persona's version doesn't feel right once you see it, that's not a failure of the process — it's the process working. Go back to the design document, adjust what you said about that persona, and the shaped version updates from there.

This is different from the old way, where changing your mind meant starting the layout over, or rewriting a prompt from nothing and hoping the new version came out consistent with the old one. Here, the design document is still the one thing you're editing. Everything downstream follows it.

## It doesn't have to be decided all at once

You don't need every persona fully worked out before anything can happen. Add a persona later. Realize a dimension matters that you hadn't considered. Notice two personas actually need the same thing and simplify. The document grows as your own understanding of who you're designing for grows — it isn't a form you fill in once and never touch again.

## The test for whether it's working

If you look at what a persona receives and can't point back to something you actually decided — a persona you named, a preference you stated — that's a sign the design document needs another pass, not that refraction did something wrong. The shaped result should always be traceable back to something you said, on purpose.

Ask it plainly, every time you review a variant: *why does this persona see this, in this order, with this much explanation?* There should be an answer, and it should be yours. "Because that's what a returning customer usually wants" is yours. "I'm not sure, that's just what came out" is the signal something's missing upstream — not a mistake in the shaping, a gap in what you actually told it.

This works the same way in reverse. If two personas end up receiving something nearly identical, ask whether that's because they genuinely need the same thing, or because you never actually said what's different about them. Sameness isn't automatically wrong — some personas really do converge. But it should be a decision you can name, not a default the system fell back to because your design document didn't distinguish them.

The habit worth building: every time something surprises you, go back to the document before you go back to the output. The output is downstream. If it's wrong, the fix is almost always upstream — in what you said, or didn't yet say — not in the shaping itself.

Over time, this becomes less of a check and more of an instinct: you'll start writing design documents that already anticipate the question, because you've seen what happens when they don't.
