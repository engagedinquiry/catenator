# The design document

You don't need to understand a schema, a descriptor, or a coordinate model to write one. You need to answer the same questions you already ask before you start any real work — just answer them somewhere that holds onto what you said.

## Start with what you already know

Before anything else, say what you're making and who it's for, in your own words. Not a form. A sentence or two, the way you'd describe it to a colleague.

*"A landing page for a returning customer who already knows our product, versus a first-time visitor who's never heard of us."*

That's it. That sentence already contains a persona (returning customer, first-time visitor), an intent (convert someone who's undecided, versus reassure someone who's already committed), and the beginning of a design document. Nothing about it needed translation.

## Make personas that govern, not the kind you're used to

If you've ever sat through a persona workshop — a stock photo, an invented name, a list of frustrations, an assumed aspiration — you've made the kind that decorates a wall, not the kind that governs anything. Hours of research produce a slide. The slide gets filed. The sprint begins, and the grid makes every actual decision, with the persona nowhere in the room.

That's not a failure of effort. It's a failure of position. A persona presented once and then set aside can only inform — passively, and only for as long as whoever saw the slide happens to remember it. It was never built to decide anything.

> A persona governs or it decorates. There is no middle position.

Here's what governing actually looks like: name the persona *before* anything else gets decided, and let every later choice answer to it. Not "returning customer" as a label on a slide — "returning customer" as the thing that determines what appears first, what tone is used, what's assumed already known versus what still needs explaining. If a layout decision can't be traced back to one of your named personas, it isn't governed — it's just something that felt right at the time, the same as before.

Write the persona first. Let the grid, the copy, the layout be a consequence of it — not something designed separately and checked against it afterward.

## Say what matters, for each version

For each persona, answer what actually changes:

- What do they need to see first?
- What do they already trust, and what do they still need convincing of?
- Does this need to look different depending on where they're seeing it — a phone, a tablet, a kiosk?
- Does it need to remember anything about them the next time they come back?

You're not filling in a form with required fields. You're doing the same thing a good creative brief always did — deciding, on purpose, what's different about this audience before you design a single screen for them.

You are creating a document describing your design or solution. That document doesn't have to be text. There are tools out there that turn a voice recording, a sketch, or a rough conversation into a written record — explore what works for how you actually think. In some cases, a sketch alone might already be enough.

## Let it hold what you said

Once you've said this, it's recorded — not as a prompt that disappears the moment it's answered, but as a design document that survives being handed to a developer, an AI system, or you in six months, having forgotten the reasoning behind today's decision.

This is the part that used to happen anyway, in a good creative process — a document that preceded the layout, that the whole team could point back to when someone asked "wait, why did we do it this way?" You're not adding a step. You're making sure the step you already did doesn't evaporate the moment it's answered.

## Watch it become different things

From the same design document, without you writing it twice:

- A developer gets a specification precise enough to build from, without guessing at what you meant.
- A stakeholder gets a document that explains the decision, in their language, without you writing a separate summary.
- A reader — or an agent reading on someone's behalf — gets an experience shaped for them specifically, not a generic version they have to make sense of alone.

You made one design document. It becomes different things depending on who's receiving it — the same way a good brief always let a copywriter, a developer, and a client each pull something different and correct from it, without anyone rewriting the document itself.

## What you don't have to do

You don't have to learn descriptors, YAML, or a coordinate model to write a design document. That's the machinery underneath — built for people who build the tools, not for the people the tools are built for. If you're curious how it actually works under the surface, it's documented, and you're welcome to it. But nothing above requires it.
